How are fats digested? Where do they get digested?

Required answer points

  • Bile emulsifies fats into small globules
  • Pancreatic lipase acts on fats
  • Digestion is completed in the small intestine
  • End products include fatty acids and glycerol

Optional answer points

  • The intestinal medium becomes alkaline

Prohibited / unsupported claims

  • Fats are completely digested in the stomach

Answer A

Fats are digested in the small intestine. The process begins with the liver secreting bile juice, which converts fats into small globule-like forms through a process called emulsification. This increases the surface area for further digestion. The pancreas then secretes pancreatic juice containing the enzyme lipase, which breaks down the fats into fatty acids and glycerol. These small molecules are then absorbed by the walls of the small intestine.

Answer A

Double circulation is a system in which blood passes through the heart twice during one complete cycle. In humans, this process is important because it ensures that blood is efficiently circulated to all parts of the body and also to the lungs for oxygenation. The double circulation consists of two parts: pulmonary circulation and systemic circulation. In pulmonary circulation, blood from the right ventricle is pumped to the lungs through the pulmonary arteries, where it picks up oxygen and releases carbon dioxide. The oxygenated blood then returns to the left atrium via the pulmonary veins. In systemic circulation, blood from the left ventricle is pumped through the aorta to the rest of the body, delivering oxygen and nutrients to tissues. The deoxygenated blood then returns to the right atrium via the veins. This double circulation ensures that the blood is continuously oxygenated and supplied with nutrients, which is essential for the proper functioning of the human body.

Harvey began thinking about the valves his teacher, Fabrici, had

to move toward the heart.

was true of all veins.

Double circulation refers to the process where blood passes through the heart twice during one complete cycle of circulation. In humans, this involves two main pathways: pulmonary circulation and systemic circulation. 1. **Pulmonary Circulation**: Blood is pumped from the right ventricle of the heart to the lungs via the pulmonary arteries. In the lungs, the blood releases carbon dioxide and picks up oxygen. The oxygenated blood then returns to the left atrium of the heart through the pulmonary veins. 2. **Systemic Circulation**: The oxygen-rich blood moves from the left atrium to the left ventricle, from where it is pumped out to the rest of the body through the aorta. As the blood circulates through the body, it delivers oxygen and nutrients to tissues and collects carbon dioxide and other waste products. The deoxygenated blood returns to the right atrium of the heart via the superior and inferior vena cava, completing the cycle. Double circulation is important in humans because it ensures efficient oxygenation of blood and separation of oxygenated and deoxygenated blood, which is crucial for maintaining high metabolic rates and supporting complex bodily functions. This system allows for a more efficient supply of oxygen to body tissues and organs, which is essential for sustaining the energy demands of the human body. Double circulation refers to the process where blood flows through the heart twice during one complete cycle of circulation. In humans, this involves two main pathways: pulmonary circulation and systemic circulation. In pulmonary circulation, blood is pumped from the right ventricle of the heart to the lungs via the pulmonary arteries. In the lungs, the blood picks up oxygen and releases carbon dioxide. The oxygen-rich blood then returns to the left atrium of the heart through the pulmonary veins. In systemic circulation, the oxygenated blood is pumped from the left ventricle into the aorta and distributed to the rest of the body. After delivering oxygen and nutrients to the tissues and collecting waste products, the deoxygenated blood returns to the right atrium of the heart through the veins. Double circulation is important in humans because it ensures efficient oxygenation of blood and separation of oxygen-rich and oxygen-poor blood, which is crucial for maintaining high metabolic rates and supporting complex bodily functions.

William Harvey (1578-1657), an English doctor, went to Italy for
further education under Fabrici.
Harvey dissected the hearts of dead people and studied the valves
between each atrium and its ventricle. He noticed that they were one-
way valves. They allowed the blood to flow from the atrium to the
ventricle without any hinderance. However, when the heart contracts,
blood in the ventricle could not flow back into the atrium. Instead, the
blood was pushed out into the arteries.
Harvey began thinking about the valves his teacher, Fabrici, had
discovered in the leg veins. They were one-way, and they forced the blood
to move toward the heart.
He checked that by tying off and blocking different veins in animals.
He found that the veins always bulged on the side of the block away from
the heart. As though the blood was trying to flow toward the heart. This
was true of all veins.
In the arteries, the blood bulged on the heart side of any block he put
in, as though it were trying to flow away from the heart and couldn’t move
in the other direction.
Harvey now saw what was happening. The heart pushed blood in to the
arteries and the blood returned by way of the veins. It did this for both
ventricles. The blood had a double circulation. Starting from the right
ventricle blood left by way of the arteries to the lungs and returned by way
of veins to the left atrium, this is known as ‘pulmonary circulation’.

rest of the body and returned by way of the veins to the right atrium. From
here it moved into the right ventricle, this is known as ‘systemic
circulation’. As blood reaches the heart two times through pulmonary
cycle and again through systemic, it is called ‘double circulation’.
